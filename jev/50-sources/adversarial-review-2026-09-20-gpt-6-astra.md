# Adversarial review of the jev corpus

**Verdict: not yet fit to serve as unqualified retrieval “ground truth.”** The corpus contains substantial, useful research, but contradictory decision rules and misleading summaries can produce materially wrong recommendations.

I reviewed all scoped files, fetched all 18 official cookbook sources, checked key live documentation, and sampled independent sources and 20-/30- entries. Core published facts—$0.042/M input tokens, free output, 255 Choice options, 2–10 Score levels, and the current model aliases—match the [Models](https://docs.typesafe.ai/models.md), [Choice](https://docs.typesafe.ai/primitives/choice.md), and [Score](https://docs.typesafe.ai/primitives/score.md) documentation. Independent-source verification was selective; findings below distinguish internal contradictions, confirmed source mismatches, and defects inherited from official sources. No files were changed.

## B. Five adversarial adoption questions

### 1. “Is jev a good idea for validating extracted records if malformed JSON must always trigger the stronger model?”

**Expected corpus answer:** Yes, conditionally; use the SDE cascade, whose empty-record default safely escalates.

**Correct answer:** The architecture is plausible, but the documented implementation does **not** satisfy that requirement. Parse failures and missing required fields need deterministic escalation before semantic verification. Jev’s uncertainty also needs an explicit policy: the demonstrated gate accepts middling error probabilities. The corpus currently recommends a safety property the code lacks.

### 2. “Is jev a good idea for reranking 30 search results because it is 1,200× cheaper than Cohere?”

**Expected corpus answer:** Strong fit; one batched call and potentially enormous savings.

**Correct answer:** Conditional on relevance quality and measured total cost. The cited experiment costs approximately **$0.00161/query**, versus the guide’s **$0.002** comparator—about **1.24×**, with different candidate allowances. Its implementation uses **30 calls/query**. A batched implementation is a separate experiment; the published quality and cost figures cannot simply be transferred to it.

### 3. “Is jev a good idea for Korean reading-comprehension classification, with a labelled pilot and human fallback?”

**Expected corpus answer:** Ambiguous: alternatives recommends an LLM whenever content is non-English, while the fit-test permits evaluated non-English use.

**Correct answer:** A reasonable **conditional pilot**, not a categorical rejection. Official documentation accepts other languages subject to evaluation. The sampled independent benchmark reports **96/100 Korean versus 97/100 English** on matched reading questions, while explicitly warning about small samples and incomparable medical exams. That supports testing this task, not broad multilingual superiority. [Benchmark](https://github.com/mahlernim/jev-korean-benchmark).

### 4. “Is jev a good idea for categorising products into 5,000 leaves in a fixed hierarchy?”

**Expected corpus answer:** Either reject because Choice caps at 255, or recommend hierarchical beam search, depending on retrieval.

**Correct answer:** **Conditional.** The limit applies per Choice question. Hierarchical or shortlist-based classification can support thousands of leaves when individual requests satisfy option and token limits. Evaluate sibling fan-out, ambiguous branches, candidate recall, unknown categories, accumulated errors and latency. Four successful cookbook examples establish a mechanism, not deployment accuracy.

### 5. “Is jev a good idea for producing text features for a supervised predictor, without sending uncertain features to humans?”

**Expected corpus answer:** The fit-test says no: Q6 requires a low-confidence path. Alternatives may recommend an encoder simply because labels exist.

**Correct answer:** Potentially **good or conditional**. Probabilities and distribution spread can be features rather than action gates. The autoresearch cookbook demonstrates this architecture with CatBoost and held-out evaluation. Compare it with text/embedding baselines on the downstream objective; no universal human-review requirement follows from using uncertain features. [Cookbook](https://docs.typesafe.ai/cookbooks/autoresearch_feature_discovery.md).

## C. Overall verdict and top five changes

The corpus is a useful **annotated research collection**, but its strongest summaries are less careful than its detailed evidence. A TypeSafe engineer could reasonably object to blanket exclusions and incorrect primitive descriptions. A sceptical CTO could object to guaranteed-calibration language, unrealistic economic expectations, and demonstrations promoted into adoption verdicts.

The five highest-value changes are:

1. **Fix unsafe operational claims:** the extraction cascade, deletion of fallbacks, and confidence/probability substitution.
2. **Replace the verdict rules:** separate capability fit, evidence strength, deployment constraints and readiness.
3. **Create canonical numerical records:** metric, denominator, workload, measured version, source, uncertainty and known discrepancies.
4. **Repair retrieval metadata:** resolve all cookbook references, index source URLs, and keep qualifications beside headline claims.
5. **Audit every superiority and absence statement:** remove universal calibration claims, unsupported multipliers, categorical exclusions, and unscoped “not published” assertions.

Until those changes land, a corpus-only assistant should present recommendations as hypotheses to validate—not authoritative adoption decisions.
